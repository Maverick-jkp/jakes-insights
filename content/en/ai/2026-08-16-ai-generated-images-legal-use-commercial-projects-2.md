The short answer is yes — but with conditions that'll catch you off guard if you skip the fine print. Licensing frameworks, copyright ambiguity, and platform-specific rules have created a patchwork that's workable but not simple. Most businesses are already using AI visuals in production. The question is whether they're doing it without inadvertently exposing themselves to risk.

Whether AI-generated images are legal to use in commercial projects in 2026 isn't a binary question anymore. It's a licensing question, a copyright question, and increasingly a disclosure question — three separate tracks that all demand attention.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - AI-generated images are commercially usable in 2026, but your legal rights flow from the tool's license — not general copyright law.
> - The U.S. Copyright Office holds that purely AI-generated images without sufficient human creative input don't qualify for copyright protection.
> - Midjourney, Leonardo AI, and ZSky AI all grant commercial rights on paid plans; terms differ significantly on free tiers.
> - Major ad platforms and stock libraries now accept AI visuals, but mandatory disclosure requirements are expanding across jurisdictions.
> - Generating commercial images featuring real, identifiable public figures remains prohibited across virtually all major platforms.

## The Legal Framework That Emerged Post-2024

Three years ago, using AI images in client work felt like operating in a grey zone. That grey zone has narrowed considerably — though it hasn't disappeared.

The U.S. Copyright Office issued clarifications in 2023 and reinforced them through 2024–2025: purely AI-generated content — where a human types a prompt and accepts the output — doesn't meet the "human authorship" threshold required for copyright protection. This matters commercially because it means *you* can't sue someone for copying your AI-generated logo. But it doesn't mean you can't *use* the image commercially. Those are different legal questions.

What governs commercial use is the **platform license**, not federal copyright. When you generate an image on Midjourney, you're operating under Midjourney's Terms of Service. Those terms define what you can do with the output. The copyright vacuum at the federal level is mostly irrelevant to your day-to-day business use case.

Two events accelerated this clarity. First, the *Thaler v. Vidal* and related U.S. court decisions between 2023–2025 consistently refused copyright protection for AI-only outputs. Second, the EU AI Act — fully enforceable as of August 2026 — introduced transparency obligations for AI-generated content used in commercial contexts, pushing companies toward explicit disclosure practices.

The practical upshot: commercial use of AI images is legal when the platform license permits it. The legal exposure most businesses face isn't from using AI images — it's from using them incorrectly.

## Platform Licenses: What You're Actually Buying

This is where the real analysis lives. Not all AI image tools grant the same rights, and "commercial use" clauses vary more than most people realize.

### The Paid Plan Threshold

Most major tools gate commercial rights behind a paid subscription. According to ZSky AI's licensing documentation, Midjourney grants commercial rights on all paid plans starting at $10/month, Leonardo AI at $12/month. ZSky AI goes further — it includes commercial rights even on its free tier, which is an outlier in the current market.

A standard commercial license typically covers:
- Marketing materials, ads, and social content
- Product mockups and e-commerce imagery
- Print-on-demand merchandise
- Client deliverables and agency work
- Post-processing and derivative edits

What it doesn't cover, almost universally:
- Training competing AI models on the output
- Generating images of real, identifiable people for commercial endorsement
- Creating deepfakes or deceptive identity content

The practical difference between these tools for commercial projects isn't image quality — it's the free tier policy and the edge cases buried in their Terms of Service. If you're running an internal prototype or MVP, ZSky's free commercial tier is a real advantage. For high-volume production with brand assets, Midjourney's ecosystem of integrations probably justifies the cost.

### Copyright Status vs. License Rights: Don't Confuse Them

A commercial license from your AI tool is sufficient for most business applications — even though the underlying image likely has no copyright owner. Think of it like open-source software: no one "owns" the code, but the license still dictates what you can do with it.

The practical implication: if you're assessing liability exposure, focus on your platform's ToS first. Federal copyright law mostly creates a gap in protection, not a restriction on use. That's an important distinction most teams miss.

## Disclosure Requirements: The Emerging Compliance Layer

This is the fastest-moving part of the landscape right now — and the one most businesses underestimate.

**Stock platforms** like Adobe Stock and Shutterstock accept AI-generated submissions as of 2026, but require mandatory AI labeling. Skip that label and your submission gets rejected — or worse, pulled after publication.

**Social platforms** have moved toward AI content labels with varying degrees of enforcement. Meta's content labeling policies, for instance, flag AI-generated imagery in political advertising contexts automatically in some regions.

**The EU AI Act** imposes transparency obligations on AI-generated content used in commercial communications targeting EU consumers. This doesn't ban the content — it requires disclosure that it was AI-generated. Non-compliance carries fines scaled to company revenue, using the same enforcement architecture as GDPR.

No universal global standard exists yet. Requirements currently vary by jurisdiction, platform, and content category. But the direction is clear: disclosure is becoming baseline compliance, not optional etiquette. Industry reports suggest enforcement will tighten significantly through late 2026 and into 2027, particularly in the EU and in U.S. political advertising contexts.

This approach can fail when teams treat disclosure as a one-time checkbox rather than a workflow. Platforms update their labeling requirements with limited notice. What was compliant in Q1 may not be compliant in Q3.

## What This Means for Different Teams Right Now

**For agencies and freelancers:** Client contracts should explicitly address AI image ownership and disclosure. If the images have no copyright owner — which is likely — you can't transfer IP you don't hold. Clarify this upfront, especially for logo work or brand identity. Assuming the client knows the legal status of AI outputs is a mistake that's created real friction in client relationships across the industry.

**For e-commerce and product teams:** AI-generated product mockups and lifestyle imagery are widely accepted by ad platforms as of mid-2026. The risk surface is narrow: avoid real people, stay on paid tiers, label appropriately for stock submissions. This isn't always the answer for brand identity work, where copyright ownership may matter downstream.

**For in-house marketing teams:** Build a disclosure workflow now. Whether AI-generated images are technically legal to use matters less than whether your team can demonstrate compliance when an ad platform or regulator asks. Document the tool used, the plan tier, and any human editing applied. That documentation trail is your defense if a submission gets flagged.

**What to watch in the next 6 months:**
- U.S. federal legislation on AI content labeling — multiple bills were in committee as of Q3 2026
- Platform-specific enforcement of AI disclosure in paid advertising, where enforcement has consistently lagged policy
- Whether any major lawsuit establishes precedent on AI image liability for commercial use

## Conclusion & Outlook

The core finding: yes, AI-generated images are legal to use in commercial projects in 2026 — under the right conditions. Platform licenses, not copyright law, are the operative framework. Disclosure requirements are the fastest-growing compliance surface, and the gap between current policy and current enforcement is closing faster than most legal teams have prepared for.

Key takeaways to carry forward:
- Paid tiers unlock commercial rights on most major tools; ZSky AI is the notable free-tier exception
- Federal copyright protection doesn't extend to purely AI-generated outputs — plan your IP strategy accordingly
- EU AI Act enforcement means disclosure isn't optional for businesses with European customers
- Real person restrictions are a hard line across every major platform

The next 12 months will bring more regulatory clarity, not less complexity. Waiting for a unified legal standard isn't a viable strategy. The smarter move is building internal workflows that can adapt as platform policies and regional regulations continue to diverge.

One concrete action to take now: audit your current AI image tools against their commercial license terms before your next client deliverable. The gap between what teams assume they're licensed to do and what the Terms of Service actually says is wider than most expect — and that gap is where liability quietly accumulates.

What disclosure practices is your team currently using for AI-generated commercial content? That's the compliance question most businesses haven't answered yet.
