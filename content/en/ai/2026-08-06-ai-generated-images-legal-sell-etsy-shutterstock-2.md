Perlmutter*, it confirmed what the Copyright Office had been signaling for two years: purely AI-generated images can't be copyrighted in the US. And yet, sellers are moving serious volume on these platforms right now.

The question of whether AI-generated images are legal to sell on Etsy or Shutterstock in 2026 doesn't have a single answer. It has a platform-specific answer, a copyright-specific answer, and a compliance-specific answer — and conflating them is where most sellers get into trouble.

Three platforms. Three completely different stories.

This analysis breaks down the legal framework, the platform-by-platform policy landscape, earnings data from active sellers, and what compliance actually looks like in practice.

## Background & Context

The legal ambiguity around AI art didn't start in 2026. The Copyright Office first signaled its position in 2023, rejecting copyright registration for Jason Allen's AI-generated piece *Théâtre D'Opéra Spatial* and granting only partial protection to Kristina Kashtanova's *Zarya of the Dawn* — covering the human-written text but not the Midjourney-generated images.

*Thaler v. Perlmutter* became the defining test case. Stephen Thaler argued his DABUS AI system should be recognized as author of a registered work. The Copyright Office said no. A federal district court agreed. The DC Circuit affirmed. And in March 2026, the Supreme Court declined cert, leaving the lower court's ruling intact.

What this means practically: prompts alone don't create copyright. Two users who type identical prompts into Midjourney both get usage rights to their respective outputs — neither owns exclusive rights. [According to ZSky AI](https://zsky.ai/blog/sell-ai-generated-content-legally), works involving substantial human creative input — editing, composition choices, curation — carry stronger copyright claims.

The platforms were moving faster than the courts. Adobe Stock built out an AI submission program with labeling requirements. Etsy updated its Creativity Standards in 2024, formally classifying AI as a tool rather than grounds for restriction. Shutterstock moved in the opposite direction, locking out AI contributor content entirely while licensing AI-generated images through its own internal programs.

The FTC also entered the picture, signaling expectations of transparency in commercial AI contexts. That pressure accelerated disclosure mandates across major platforms through 2025 and into 2026.

## Main Analysis

### The Copyright Floor: What "No Copyright" Actually Means for Sellers

No copyright doesn't mean no sales. It means no exclusive ownership.

The Copyright Office has already granted registrations for AI-assisted works where humans made substantial creative contributions — composition adjustments, Photoshop edits, element combinations, styling decisions. The threshold isn't binary. A raw Midjourney output? Likely unprotectable. That same output refined with manual layer edits, color grading, and element recomposition? Potentially protectable, case by case.

[According to AutoKeyWorder](https://autokeyworder.com/blog/sell-ai-generated-art/), 51 active copyright lawsuits currently target AI companies, with *Andersen v. Stability AI* set for trial in September 2026. That trial will likely clarify whether training data usage constitutes infringement — which could affect the underlying models sellers depend on. Worth tracking closely.

The three legal risk zones [according to ZSky AI](https://zsky.ai/blog/sell-ai-generated-content-legally) are well-defined: generating recognizable likenesses of real people (right of publicity), recreating copyrighted characters or brand assets, and failing to disclose AI generation where legally required. The first two represent genuine legal exposure. The third is primarily a platform compliance issue.

### Platform-by-Platform: The Actual Rules in 2026

Etsy's position is the most nuanced. Its 2024 Creativity Standards permit AI-generated digital products under three conditions: disclosure in the listing description, genuine originality, and correct categorization. [According to PromptlessPress](https://www.promptlesspress.com/blog-can-you-use-ai-to-sell-on-etsy), the originality threshold requires "concept development, curation, composition adjustments, and styling." Generating generic output and listing it unchanged fails the standard. If a buyer could find the identical product in another shop from the same undifferentiated prompt, more creative input is needed.

Shutterstock's position is the hardest line in the industry. No AI-generated contributor submissions. Full stop. It operates its own AI image licensing program internally but keeps contributor-submitted AI content off the table entirely.

Adobe Stock sits at the opposite end — already 47.85% AI-generated and growing. The submission process requires a disclosure checkbox and commercial rights confirmation. Royalty rate: 33%. New accounts face an upload cap of roughly 51 images per week.

Adobe Stock is the volume play. Etsy is the niche product play. Redbubble suits low-effort print-on-demand. Shutterstock is not in the equation for AI contributors — at least not in 2026.

The trade-off between Etsy and Adobe Stock is meaningful. Etsy requires more upfront creative work per listing — 13 tags, a disclosure statement, genuine differentiation — but each sale can earn $3–$25 on a single digital download. Adobe Stock rewards volume: more uploads, more keyword coverage, more downloads at 33% royalty. [According to AutoKeyWorder](https://autokeyworder.com/blog/sell-ai-generated-art/), one seller generated $3,976 in their first year on Adobe Stock alone, but median monthly earnings across platforms sit at $200–$800 for active sellers.

Metadata drives visibility more than image quality on both platforms. Adobe Stock allows 49 keywords per submission. Etsy permits 13 tags. Sellers who research search demand before generating consistently outperform those who upload first and optimize later — one documented case showed monthly earnings jumping from under $20 to over $200 within two months after switching to a research-first approach.

### The Human-Touch Multiplier

Adding human creative editing isn't just about aesthetics. It's the mechanism that turns unprotectable AI output into something with genuine copyright claims and real market differentiation.

Two sellers using identical Midjourney prompts produce identical baseline outputs. The one who edits color grading in Photoshop, adjusts composition, removes artifacts, and adds original text elements has a product that's both more legally defensible and harder to replicate. That's the gap between $2/month and $200/month — not the AI model, but the work layered on top of it.

This approach can fail when sellers treat light editing as a checkbox rather than a genuine creative step. Cropping an image or adjusting brightness slightly won't meet Etsy's originality standard or strengthen a copyright claim. The editing has to be substantial enough that the final product reflects independent creative decisions.

## Practical Implications

**For sellers deciding which platform to start on:** Etsy's barrier is creative input. Adobe Stock's barrier is volume and keyword research. Pick the constraint that fits your workflow. Don't start on Shutterstock expecting to list AI work — it's wasted effort in 2026.

**Scenario 1 — Digital downloads on Etsy:** Disclose AI use explicitly in every listing description. No template required, but it must be unambiguous. Build a library of genuinely differentiated products — niche clipart sets, themed planner pages, style-specific illustrations — rather than generic outputs. Etsy's removal triggers are disclosure violations and design copying, not AI use itself.

**Scenario 2 — Stock photography on Adobe:** Front-load keyword research. Adobe Stock's search algorithm surfaces images based on metadata quality, not visual quality alone. Use all 49 available keywords. Check existing search volume for your niche before generating. The $3,976 first-year result documented by AutoKeyWorder came from a research-first workflow, not mass uploading.

**Scenario 3 — Print-on-demand via Redbubble:** Keep designs clear of copyrighted characters, logos, and sports team branding. The AI checkbox is all that's required for disclosure. New account upload limits (5/day) mean a slow build — plan for a 3–6 month runway before meaningful earnings.

**What to watch next:** The *Andersen v. Stability AI* trial in September 2026 could materially affect the legal standing of outputs from models trained on scraped data. A ruling against Stability AI could trigger platform policy shifts across Adobe Stock, Freepik, and others that currently accept AI submissions. That's the single biggest legal signal to track before year-end.

## Conclusion & Future Outlook

The legal picture is clearer than most coverage suggests.

Selling AI art is legal. No copyright protection on raw outputs doesn't equal illegality. Etsy accepts AI with conditions — disclosure, originality, and correct categorization are non-negotiable. Shutterstock is closed to AI contributors, and that position hasn't shifted in 2026. Human editing is both a legal and commercial strategy. It's the variable that separates defensible work from commodity output.

The next 6–12 months hinge on one development: the *Andersen v. Stability AI* trial outcome. A ruling against Stability AI could trigger platform-wide policy reviews and model access restrictions. A ruling in their favor normalizes the current landscape through at least 2027.

For sellers, the practical move isn't waiting for legal clarity — it's building workflows that would survive either outcome. Disclose consistently. Edit substantially. Research before generating. And don't build a business entirely dependent on a single platform's current policy.

The market's moving fast. The compliance framework is already visible. The sellers doing well right now aren't gambling on legal gray areas — they're just reading the policies correctly.
