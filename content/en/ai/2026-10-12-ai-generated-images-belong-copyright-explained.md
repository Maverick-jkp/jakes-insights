Most creators assume ownership over whatever they make. Type a prompt, get an image, it's yours — right?

Not under U.S. law. And the gap between that assumption and legal reality is costing people real money in 2026.

The short version: the U.S. Copyright Office doesn't recognize AI as an author. A purely AI-generated image sits in a legal grey zone where *nobody* owns it — including you. But the full picture is messier than that one sentence suggests, and the court system is actively rewriting the rules as you read this.

If you're building products, creating content, or licensing AI-generated assets professionally, this isn't abstract legal trivia. It's a direct operational risk.

---

> **In brief:** U.S. copyright law explicitly excludes non-human authors, leaving purely AI-generated images unprotected. Meaningful human modification can create protectable derivative works — and courts are drawing that line case by case right now.
>
> - A purely AI-generated image carries no copyright, meaning anyone can reproduce it without your permission.
> - Human creative modifications that cross the "originality threshold" under 17 U.S.C. § 106(2) can produce protectable derivative works.
> - The $1.5 billion Anthropic settlement in 2025 signals that copyright litigation against AI companies is no longer theoretical — it's expensive and accelerating.

## How Copyright Law Got Left Behind

Copyright law was designed for humans. The Copyright Act of 1976 — still the operative framework — anchors protection in human authorship. The U.S. Copyright Office's position is explicit: works created by non-humans receive no copyright protection. Full stop. That was a manageable doctrine when "non-human creation" meant edge cases.

Generative AI broke the edge case into a flood.

The 2022 *Zarya of the Dawn* case was the first major public test. The Copyright Office granted protection to graphic novelist Kristina Kashtanova's text and story structure, but stripped protection from the Midjourney-generated images embedded in the work. The rationale: text prompts producing "unpredictable outputs" don't constitute human authorship in the legal sense. That precedent has held.

Since then, litigation has escalated sharply. Getty Images sued Stability AI for unauthorized use of millions of copyrighted photos to train image models. The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in 2023 for training data scraping, then targeted Perplexity in 2025. Universal Music and major labels pursued Anthropic and AI music startups Suno and Uncharted Labs for similar reasons.

The watershed moment came in June 2025. A federal judge ruled that AI companies can legally train on legitimately obtained copyrighted works under fair use — but found that Anthropic's acquisition methods constituted piracy. The result: a **$1.5 billion settlement**, the largest copyright payout in U.S. history, averaging roughly $3,000 per pirated work, [according to Built In's analysis of AI copyright developments](https://builtin.com/artificial-intelligence/ai-copyright).

The legal landscape isn't settled. It's actively being written.

## The Baseline Rule: No Human, No Copyright

The foundational rule is blunt. A purely AI-generated image — no human modification, just a prompt and an output — belongs to nobody. You can't prevent a competitor from downloading your Midjourney output and using it on their product page. You have no legal recourse.

This creates an immediate commercial problem. If you're using AI-generated images in marketing materials, product mockups, or content pipelines and treating them as proprietary assets, that assumption is legally wrong. Anyone can replicate or redistribute those images freely.

The practical risk cuts both ways. Download what appears to be an AI-generated image from a third-party source, and if it turns out a human meaningfully modified it before upload, you may be infringing on that person's copyright — even if you acted in good faith. [According to Blue Media's copyright analysis](https://www.gobluemedia.com/blog/ai-generated-image-copyright/), the burden of proving AI origin falls on the user, not the claimant. Without access to the source file, that's an almost impossible standard to meet.

## The Derivative Works Exception: Where Humans Get Their Rights Back

Not everything is lost. Under **17 U.S.C. § 106(2)**, derivative works that achieve sufficient originality can qualify for independent copyright protection. The key word is "sufficient."

When a human meaningfully transforms an AI-generated image — through substantial colorization, compositional restructuring, manual painting over the output, or significant filtering — the resulting work may cross the originality threshold and become copyrightable human authorship. The AI-generated base doesn't disqualify the final product.

Vanderbilt Law professor Daniel Gervais, writing in his 2022 *Seton Hall Law Review* paper, raised a related unresolved question: whether infringing a derivative work right requires the derivative itself to be original. Legal experts currently lean toward the underlying work not needing human origin for a human-modified derivative to qualify for protection. That question is still working through the courts.

The practical implication is straightforward. The more demonstrably human your creative intervention, the stronger your copyright claim. Document your modifications. Keep version histories. Maintain records of what changed between the raw AI output and your final asset.

## The Proof Problem: Practical Risk in the Real World

Even where the law is theoretically clear, proving facts is hard.

Imagine using an AI-generated image commercially, confident it's uncopyrightable. A claimant appears with a high-resolution source file and argues human authorship. Now you need to prove the image was purely AI-generated — without access to the original prompt, the model version, or any generation metadata. Courts don't resolve ambiguity in your favor by default.

This isn't hypothetical paranoia. As AI image generators proliferate and outputs become increasingly indistinguishable from human work, verification becomes structurally difficult. Watermarking systems like those proposed by the Content Authenticity Initiative (CAI) are being adopted by Adobe and others, but they're not universal and not tamper-proof.

No option is perfect. Pure AI generation is fast and cheap but legally naked. Human modification creates some protection but requires process documentation to defend. Licensed stock and original human work offer cleaner legal positions at higher cost and time investment.

## Who's Actually Exposed — and How

**Content creators and solo operators** are the most immediately exposed. Many are treating AI image outputs as owned assets — building brand identities, selling digital products, licensing derivatives — on a legal foundation that doesn't exist. The fix isn't complicated: document every meaningful human modification, source from platforms with explicit licensing terms, and stop treating raw AI outputs as proprietary.

**Product and engineering teams** integrating AI image generation into software pipelines face a different problem. If your product generates images for users and those users assume copyright ownership, you're potentially setting them up for a legal misunderstanding. Clear terms of service that accurately describe the copyright status of AI outputs aren't optional — they're a liability management requirement.

**Enterprise legal and compliance teams** need to watch two specific developments. First, the outcome of the Getty vs. Stability AI suit will clarify whether training-data sourcing creates downstream liability for image outputs. Second, any legislative movement on the proposed AI transparency and copyright frameworks circulating in the EU and U.S. Congress in 2026 could redefine what "meaningful human authorship" requires as a documentation standard.

This approach can fail when teams assume that using a paid AI image tool automatically transfers ownership. It doesn't. Platform terms govern commercial use rights — not copyright law — and those terms vary significantly across Midjourney, Adobe Firefly, DALL·E, and others. Read them before you build around them.

One development worth tracking: if the Copyright Office finalizes its proposed AI registration guidance — expected in late 2026 — it will likely establish minimum thresholds for human creative contribution. That guidance will either validate current industry practices or force significant workflow changes across every team using generative image tools commercially.

---

## Where This Is Heading

The current state, plainly: AI-generated images belong to nobody by default. Human modification can create protectable rights, but courts are still drawing the line on what "meaningful" means. And the litigation environment — $1.5 billion settlements, active class-action suits against Midjourney and Stability AI, ongoing music industry battles — makes clear this isn't a niche legal question anymore.

> **Key Takeaways:**
> - Purely AI-generated images carry zero copyright protection under U.S. law
> - Meaningful human modification can establish derivative work protection under 17 U.S.C. § 106(2)
> - In any dispute, the proof burden falls on users — not claimants
> - Litigation is accelerating fast; the Anthropic settlement set a $1.5B precedent that won't be ignored

Over the next 6 to 12 months, watch for the Copyright Office's AI registration guidance and final rulings in the Getty/Stability AI suit. Either could materially shift how teams document and defend AI-assisted creative work.

The practical action is simple, even if the law isn't. Document everything. Know the difference between using AI as a creative tool — where you modify substantially — and delegating creation entirely, where you own nothing. That distinction, not the technology itself, is what determines your legal standing.
